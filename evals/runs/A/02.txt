This study adopts Bourdieu's theory of cultural capital as its theoretical framework.[^1] According to Bourdieu, cultural capital exists in three states: the embodied state, the objectified state, and the institutionalized state.[^2] The embodied state refers to long-lasting dispositions of mind and body; the objectified state to cultural goods such as books and instruments; and the institutionalized state to academic qualifications. This threefold distinction is highly valuable for the present study because it allows us to distinguish between what a family possesses and what a child actually acquires. We focus primarily on parents' embodied cultural capital and its influence on children's reading.

[^1]: Pierre Bourdieu, "The Forms of Capital," in *Handbook of Theory and Research for the Sociology of Education*, ed. John G. Richardson (New York: Greenwood, 1986), 241–258.
[^2]: Bourdieu, "The Forms of Capital," 243.
